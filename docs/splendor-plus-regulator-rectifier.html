Smart Regulator Rectifier

A microcontroller-controlled replacement for the stock 4-pin regulator rectifier. It keeps the original plug and the half-wave layout of the bike, and adds measured, temperature-compensated charging, true-RMS headlamp regulation, a hardware over-voltage cut-out and a proper thermal path.

Power stage detail

Sheet 2 of 8

The stator winding is earthed at one end, so each tap can only be used on one half of the AC wave. SCR1 passes positive half-cycles from the white wire to the battery and simply stays off when the battery is full, so the charging coil is never shorted. Triac TR1 trims the headlamp voltage by shunting the end of each half-cycle, positive and negative, on the yellow wire. Both gates are fired through opto-isolators by the microcontroller.

Voltage and current control

Sheet 3 of 8 · varying load

Two loops run one inside the other. The outer loop compares battery voltage with the set point and asks for a current. The inner loop compares that request with the current measured at RS1 and decides how many of every 16 positive half-cycles SCR1 passes. Because the lamps and horn join the red wire outside the unit, RS1 reads battery and load current together, so a load switching on is seen and answered within a few cycles.

Constant voltage

While the load is within reach, the clamp does nothing. The voltage loop raises or lowers the current request to match whatever the bike is drawing and the battery stays at 14.40 V.

Constant current

When the request hits the limit, output is held at 4 A. The battery supplies the shortfall, its voltage sags, and it is recharged as soon as the load drops. The limit is lowered when the heat sink is hot.

No wind-up, no overshoot

The integrator stops accumulating while the clamp is active, so the loop leaves current limit cleanly. If voltage ever runs away, U7 cuts the gate drive at 15.2 V regardless of the loops.

Battery temperature and overcharge protection

Sheet 4 of 8 · protection detail

Overcharging is guarded in layers, and each layer works without the one above it. Firmware adjusts the charge voltage to the battery's temperature. Two hardware detectors, one for voltage and one for battery temperature, can each stop the gate drive to SCR1 whatever the firmware is doing. If SCR1 itself fails short, a crowbar diverts the stator and the fuse disconnects the battery.

Battery temperature sensing

NTC2 is taped to the battery case and plugs into J2. With R8 it forms a divider that the MCU reads through a small filter. The divider is powered only while the engine runs, so it adds nothing to parked drain. A reading stuck at either rail means a broken or shorted sensor: the charge voltage then falls back to a fixed 14.1 V and LED1 shows a fault code.

Hardware inhibit

U7 (voltage) and U8 (battery temperature) have open-drain outputs tied to the gate of Q3. Either one pulling low switches Q3 off, which removes current from the opto LED, so SCR1 cannot be fired. The MCU reads the same line to log which detector tripped. A shorted or unpowered sensor reads as hot, so that fault also stops charging.

Crowbar

All of the above depends on SCR1 being able to switch off. If it fails short or fires by itself, the rail climbs. Once it stays above about 16.7 V, DZ2 charges C4 through R17 and fires SCR3, which shunts the stator to ground. The few milliseconds of delay stop normal charging ripple from tripping it. The battery then blows F1 through SCR3 and is disconnected. The bike keeps running and LED1 flags the fault.

Temperature thresholds suit a flooded or sealed lead-acid battery. In the circuit test, SCR3 carried a 255 A peak and F1 opened after 175 ms. The fuse melts at about 70 A²s, below the I²t rating of a 25 A TO-220 SCR, but check this against the datasheet of the part you buy.

Smart control

Firmware behaviour
  1. WakeThe MCU sleeps until it sees zero-crossings on the white wire. It then powers the reference and the current amplifier and checks battery voltage and both temperatures.
  2. Soft startFor the first two seconds SCR1 fires on one cycle in four, so a cold engine at idle is not loaded with full charging current.
  3. BulkSCR1 fires on every positive half-cycle. Current is read through RS1 and capped at 4 A by skipping cycles.
  4. AbsorptionA PI loop holds 14.40 V, shifted by −18 mV per °C of battery temperature, by choosing how many cycles to pass.
  5. FloatWhen current stays under 0.3 A for ten minutes the target drops to 13.6 V, which stops a small battery from gassing on long rides.

Headlamp loop

The yellow wire is sampled about twenty times per cycle and the true RMS value is computed every cycle. The MCU moves the firing point of TR1 in both half-cycles until the lamp sees 13.2 V RMS, at idle and at full revs alike. Bulbs stop dimming at idle and stop blowing at high speed.

Where the accuracy comes from

Battery voltage is taken by a separate Kelvin trace from the red pin through a 0.1 % divider and measured against a 0.1 % 2.500 V reference. Sixty-four samples are averaged per reading, giving about 4 mV resolution. The stock regulator relies on a zener that drifts by several hundred millivolts with heat.

Diagnostics and limp modes

LED1 blinks a code for each fault: over-voltage, over-temperature, open battery, blown fuse, no stator output. If the battery is unplugged, the ripple jump is seen within 3 ms and SCR1 switches to phase-angle control at 13.8 V, so the horn and lamps keep working. If the output is shorted, charging stops after 100 ms below 8 V and retries every 2 s. J2 streams voltage, current, rpm and temperatures over UART.

Board layout

Sheet 5 of 8 · top view

Revision C follows the circuit tests. TR1, a triac, takes the place of SCR2 on the wall. C2 and TVS2 now sit on the regulator side of F1, fed by a wide bottom-layer track from the node between F1 and RS1. D2 is in series with the SCR1 gate, R17 and C4 slow the crowbar trigger, and C5 filters the U7 divider.

The power devices sit on the top edge and bolt to the case wall. Stator AC, which can spike past 100 V with a lamp blown, stays in the upper half. The logic lives behind a milled slot in the lower right, and only the two opto-isolators and the 220 kΩ sense resistors bridge it.

Heat dissipation

Sheet 6 of 8 · section through SCR1

Less heat to begin with

The stock unit regulates charging by shorting the coil and burning the surplus. Here the charge SCR is in series and stays off when no charge is needed, so it only heats while it is doing useful work. In the circuit tests SCR1 reaches 5.5 W at redline with every load on, and TR1 reaches 6 W when it shunts a light lamp load at high revs.

A short path out

Junction to tab is about 2 °C/W and the insulating pad 1 °C/W. The finned case must reach 3 °C/W or better to air, about twice the fin area first planned. With up to 10 W shared between SCR1 and TR1 and 70 °C air around the unit, the hottest junction reaches 116 °C, inside the 125 °C rating. Mount the fins where air moves, not behind the side panel foam.

Watched by a sensor

NTC1 is clamped to the wall beside TR1. Charging current is reduced from 95 °C and stopped at 115 °C. Headlamp regulation keeps running, because switching it off would overvolt the bulbs.

How far to trust this. The stator is modelled as two coils, each an EMF proportional to rpm behind 1.4 to 1.6 mH, with 6 pole pairs. These are typical values for a 100 cc magneto, not measurements from a Splendor. The SCRs use a two-transistor latching model. The firmware is modelled as behaviour, not code. The battery's slow polarisation is compressed into 0.125 s so a 1 s run shows what takes minutes on the road, so read the time axes as compressed. Junction temperatures assume 70 °C air, 3 °C/W from junction to case wall and 3 °C/W from the wall to air.

The test bench is in the repository as sim/testbench.py. Running it rebuilds all 21 netlists, runs them and writes sim/results.json. Before building hardware, measure the real stator's open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current at three engine speeds, put them into the model and run it again.

Full-wave bridge option

Sheet 8 of 8 · Rev C-FW · SPICE tested

The stock 2001 Splendor charges on half the AC wave because one end of the charge coil is joined to the stator core. A full-wave bridge uses both halves, so the same coil gives roughly twice the charging current at low and middle revs. This version is a half-controlled bridge: SCR1 and a new SCR4 on top switch each half-cycle into the battery, and a dual Schottky diode D3 completes the path on the bottom. Everything else on sheet 1 stays as it is, including the lamp regulator, the protection and the firmware loops.

The stator has to be rewired. Lift the end of the charge winding that is soldered or crimped to the stator core, insulate it, and bring it out on a fifth wire to a new pin J1-5. Check with a meter that neither charge wire reads continuity to the frame. The lighting coil stays as it is. Without this change the bridge's bottom diode on the grounded end is shorted, and the circuit behaves as the half-wave design with extra losses.

What it adds

SCR4 (S8025L), D3 (MBR20200CT, 200 V dual Schottky), U9 with R22 and D4 for the second gate, R23 for the U9 LED, snubber R24 and C8, TVS4 on the second coil wire, R28 for its zero-cross sense, and a fifth pin on J1. Five devices now share the case wall, so the fins must reach 2.2 °C/W, about a third more area than the half-wave case.

What the tests showed

With the battery at its set point the bridge gives no extra heat, because the firmware fires fewer cycles. Under heavy load it carries 0.5 A more within the same junction limits and keeps the battery charging where the half-wave design drains it. Its weak spot is a shorted SCR, which no longer blows the fuse by itself. The gate-off current check on U2 covers that case.

Firmware notes

Fire each SCR only on its own half-cycle, so the coil is never shorted through both legs. Alternate which leg gets the first firing after a skip, so SCR1 and SCR4 share the heat: in T05 the model's simple priority loaded SCR1 with 3.3 W and SCR4 with 0.5 W. Read zero-cross from the difference between ZC-W1 and ZC-W2.

Before you build

Read first

This is a design concept. It has been worked through on paper and has not been built or bench-tested, so treat component values and thermal figures as starting points to verify.

Wire colours follow the usual Hero Honda 4-pin pattern: white for the charge tap, yellow for lighting, red for battery and green for ground. Harnesses vary by year and by earlier repairs, so confirm each wire with a multimeter before connecting anything.

For a first power-up, run it from the bike with a fused battery lead and a 35 W bulb on the yellow wire, and watch the battery voltage and the case temperature through a full rev range.
